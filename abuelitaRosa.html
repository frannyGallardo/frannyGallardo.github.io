Photography Calligraphy

First Drafted 22 September 2026

---

I wake up to the crow of a rooster and the early morning sun beaming through two windows a the top of the door. I don't remember if the neighbor's dogs were quarreling with those that live on the street, but it wouldn't have been out of place.

I'm not in my usual twin bed. Hell, I'm not even in my usual bedroom back in The States. I'm at the edge of the queen bed in the upstairs room of my grandma's house in Mexico: my mom's childhood home, where my aunts Marta and Rosy live, where my Uncle “Loy” (no one calls him Roberto) lives. My brother's still fast asleep in the middle of the bed and the spot at the opposite end where my dad had fallen asleep is empty.

My mind is a groggy mess, but the timeline adds up in my head. My mom called me in tears late Friday night while I was out and about to tell me Grandma had passed away, I rushed to pick up my brother from the train station, huddled around my PC to buy tickets as soon as we got home, skipped sleeping and left for O'Hare before the sun rose on Saturday, met up in Houston with my cousin, Ceci, for our now delayed flight to Aguascalientes, got picked up and driven to the wake in La Chona by my uncles “Tolo” and Martín, walked as a procession to her mass, walked again as a procession to place her in her crypt at the cemetery, got a ride back to my grandma's house from Uncle Loy and one of his friends, then stuffed myself with tamales brought by the local nuns while surrounded by my cousins, nephews, aunts and uncles, before retiring to bed a while after the clock struck midnight.

The timeline adds up in my head, but waking up here feels surreal. Practically every day of summer vacation up until the end of high school was spent in my mom's hometown, so unless I was sharing a bed with my cousins while sleeping over at my aunt “Toto's” house, I'd always wake up in the exact same bed I'm currently laying in. But a combination of college, then work, and a growing sense that I identified more with the friends I'd made at home than the family I'd inherited meant it had been at least eight years since I'd woken up in this place. The rooms had been slightly reorganized, but I otherwise recognized every nook and cranny of the home my grandpa had built. La Chona had grown pretty significantly, but if asked I could still navigate to Don Lalo's abarrote, to church for mass, to the central plaza, to my aunt Toto's house, all on my lonesome. I hadn't seen all of this family in ages, but after greeting them all with a hug or a kiss on the cheek, conversations effortlessly picked up where they'd left off long ago.

So, why do I feel foreign? Seemingly out of place? Why doesn't this feel possible? Feel real?

I know myself well enough to know there's no use trying to go back to sleep. I step foot out of the bedroom and I'm greeted by the warmth of the Mexican sun, the sound of birds also starting their day, and a familiar view of my mom's hometown beyond the terrace rooftop. I think I hear my dad cackling in the distance as I head downstairs into the zaguan. My mom says good morning from inside the bedroom closest to the front door and confirms my dad and uncle Loy are “philosophizing” out on the street corner. I join the Socratic seminar as my dad is explaining how we have Hitler and American embargoes to thank for the creation of Fanta. I'm still waking up and don't have much to say, so I diligently take mental notes as the conversation evolves from Fanta to the war in Gaza, to genealogy, to travel stories, to simply shootin' the shit.

My dad finds a spot to tag me in when the conversation shifts to the reason we are in Mexico in the first place, how it'd been a while since I'd been to visit family, how I'd ultimately grown up in a place and culture that's totally different to the one I was in right now. He asked me, “What do you make of this whole situation? How do you feel about the fact that you're here right now?”

I gave myself a second to collect my thoughts. Only a single word bubbled up to the surface of my mind:


“Agridulce”. Bittersweet.


Bitter because I felt guilty I hadn't made time to visit earlier when I was able to do so on such short notice not even 24 hours ago.

Bitter because I don't feel like I knew my grandma, maybe didn't even have the time to really get to know her.

Bitter because my cousin Andrés' premonition came true, that the next time we would see each other would be for my grandma's passing...

Sweet because I was able to visit on such short notice, that I was able accompany my mom knowing family means the world to her.

Sweet because my family and Mexico welcomed me again with such open hearts and genuine generosity.

Sweet because La Chona could still feel like home, even after all this time.

Bittersweet because it's not just bitter and sweet at the same time, but a different feeling altogether. Complicated and untangle-able. Sobering, while at the same time, warm.

---

My mom stayed back in Mexico to be with everyone for the novena. The rest of us got back to Chicago late enough on Monday that my brother couldn't take his Amtrak back to Champaign, so my dad and I decided to split up the drive to drop him off.

While I was piloting on the drive down and co-pilot on the way back home, I kept morale up by serving as DJ for the trip. I figured keeping my dad awake would be top priority, so I concocted the perfect set of tunes for the six hour trek: Genesis on the way down, Pink Floyd and Yanni on the way back, all padded with tunes from my “oldies but goodies” playlist on Spotify.

The trip was pretty quiet (barring the the occasional head-bopping sing-along), but my dad made a comment on the way back that stuck out to me:

“When I used to travel for work, I'd come back home and be surprised no one would notice that I'd just been abroad. I could have just arrived from France, the Netherlands, somewhere in South America, but everyone would greet me as if it were a regular day in the office. Seems there's nothing that physically marks you as such, as having just traveled, even after you've experienced something totally new.

“But the more I think about about it, I'm sure everyone else is experiencing that exact same thing...”
